4) What is a Transformer?

Likely interviewer question:
“What is a Transformer?”
possibly followed by
“Which Transformer?” or “Are all Transformers the same?”

Your answer, organized:

  • You referred to the 2017 Google paper.

  • You said not every Transformer is the same and different organizations make modifications.

  • You mentioned Meta, Google, OpenAI, etc. modifying the architecture.

  • You said Transformer is open and modifiable, though you were unsure about patent/licensing details.

Critique:
This part was weak. Not because you lacked all knowledge, but because the answer drifted. The interviewer probably expected a technical explanation, something like self-attention, sequence modeling, parallelization, encoder/decoder, or why it replaced RNN/LSTM in many tasks. Instead, your answer moved toward licensing, patents, and company modifications. That likely made you sound unsure.

The line “I need to check if it is patented by Google or how it’s licensed” hurt the answer. In an interview, this sounds like you lost the thread of the question.

Better version:
“A Transformer is a neural network architecture introduced in the 2017 ‘Attention Is All You Need’ paper. Its key idea is self-attention, which lets the model weigh relationships between tokens in a sequence more effectively than older sequential architectures like RNNs. That made it much better for large-scale language tasks and parallel training. Modern LLMs are Transformer-based, though different organizations adapt the base architecture in different ways.”

That would have been a strong, clean answer.

6) Explain decision tree vs random forest

Likely interviewer question:
“What is a decision tree?”
or
“What is the difference between a decision tree and random forest?”

Your answer, organized:

  • You said decision tree is a building block of random forest.

  • You described decision tree as data structured via nodes and edges.

  • You said random forest is an ensemble of many decision trees.

  • You said the final decision is based on the constituent trees’ outputs.

Critique:
The core idea was correct, but the wording was imprecise. “Hard or soft working” was unclear. Also, “data is structured in the form of nodes and edges” is not the best way to explain a decision tree in an interview. Better to explain splits, impurity reduction, overfitting, and aggregation.

Better version:
“A decision tree makes predictions by recursively splitting the data based on feature values. It is easy to interpret but can overfit. A random forest reduces that risk by combining many decision trees trained on bootstrapped samples and random subsets of features. The final output is aggregated across trees, usually by majority vote in classification or averaging in regression.”

That sounds much more interview-ready.

Overall assessment of your interview performance

You came across as:

  • honest,

  • experienced,

  • broad in exposure,

  • genuinely hands-on in applied AI/ML work,

  • not pretending to know things you do not know.

What hurt you most

These were the main issues:

1. Too much hedging and filler language
You said “yeah,” “I mean,” and similar fillers a lot. That makes answers sound less authoritative.

2. You led with your limitations too often
Instead of briefly acknowledging a gap and pivoting to strengths, you stayed too long on what you did not know.

3. Conceptual answers were not crisp enough
Transformer, decision tree, random forest, and possibly tree depth were answered in a way that felt partially correct but not interview-polished.

4. You sounded more like an API user than an architect/expert
For a senior candidate, “I use scikit-learn APIs” and “I use OpenAI API / Gemini API” are not enough by themselves. The interviewer wants design thinking, trade-offs, production considerations, evaluation, and decision rationale.
